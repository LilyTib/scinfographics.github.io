Why Yaks Thrive in Thin Air

On the Tibetan Plateau, a shaggy relative of the cow lives where the air is thin, the winters are long and cold, and food is scarce for roughly half the year. Researchers have pieced together part of the answer to how it manages, and how cattle differ.

A hard place to live

The yak’s home is the Qinghai–Tibetan Plateau, where it grazes at roughly 3,000 to 6,000 meters, or about 10,000 to 20,000 feet.

Three things make that altitude hard on a large animal. The air holds less oxygen. The temperature falls about 6 degrees Celsius, or 11 degrees Fahrenheit, for every kilometer you climb, and the cold season lasts about six months. And in late winter and early spring, food runs short and snow covers the ground, so animals lose weight.

Here is how a yak handles each challenge, and how cattle compare.

1. Low oxygen

Start with the air. Each breath at that altitude carries less oxygen, and the yak’s body answers in two places: the lungs and heart, and the blood and arteries.

A yak’s lungs and heart are relatively large compared with those of cattle raised at lower altitudes. Inside the lungs, it has more air-sac surface for its size and a thinner layer separating air from blood. Cattle at lower altitudes are smaller on each count.

The blood carries more of the oxygen, too. Yaks have higher concentrations of red blood cells and of hemoglobin, the protein that holds oxygen, than cattle do. The arteries that carry blood through a yak’s lungs have thin walls and little muscle, and the right side of its heart, which pumps blood to the lungs, shows no thickening. Cattle have more muscle in those artery walls.

In contrast, some cattle develop brisket disease, also called high-mountain disease. Fluid swelling runs from the jaw down through the brisket, the chest beneath the neck. The illness raises the pressure in the lungs. The right side of the heart has to work harder against that pressure, and eventually the heart fails.

Researchers consider natural selection the probable cause of these yak traits, but that is not proven. 2. Limited food in winter

Winter is the hard season. Food is scarce for roughly half the year, and late winter and early spring are the leanest stretch. A yak’s daily intake drops from 18 to 25 kilograms of fresh forage in summer to 6 to 8 kilograms of wilted grass in the cold season. Without extra feed, yaks lost much weight in late winter and spring.

A yak’s mouth and stomach are built for tough highland forage. Its tongue is shorter than a cow’s, with larger, more numerous bumps and a thicker lining, which lets it graze a wider range of plants. Its big stomach chamber, the rumen, takes in large loads of low-quality food and ferments it for longer to extract more nutrients. Microbes in the rumen break down about 70 to 80 percent of what a yak eats, and the fatty acids they produce supply 60 to 75 percent of its energy.

Yaks also need less just to stay alive. In a feeding experiment with six yaks and six cattle of a Chinese breed called Qaidam, yaks had lower energy requirements for maintenance than cattle, about a quarter lower. The authors say this lower requirement gives yaks an advantage over cattle in consuming low-energy diets during the long winter on the Qinghai–Tibetan Plateau.

The rumen percentages come from one study cited in the review. The daily-intake figures come from an FAO chapter. The maintenance-energy comparison comes from a 2022 experiment with small groups of animals, and it compares yaks with one cattle breed.

3. Cold climate

Then there is the cold.

A yak is compact, with relatively little skin surface for its weight, and it wears a thick coat: long outer hair over a dense layer of fine down that appears for the colder season and repels moisture. It has no working sweat glands, which the review says helps it tolerate cold.

A cow’s ability to handle cold depends on the state of its coat. A resting cow starts burning extra energy below about 15 degrees Celsius (59 Fahrenheit) with a wet or summer coat, 7 (45) with a dry fall coat, 0 (32) with a dry winter coat, and minus 8 (18) with a dry, heavy winter coat. Wind, wet hide and no shelter make it worse, and a layer of fat helps insulate.

Below that point, a cow’s energy needs climb about 1 percent for every degree Fahrenheit, and cattle eat roughly 2 to 25 percent more to make up for it. Yaks generally eat less than cattle and burn energy more slowly, though they, too, lose weight on sparse winter forage. The review lists one more cold response in yaks, the tightening of blood vessels near the skin to hold in heat, but describes it as a general reaction to cold, not something unique to yaks.

The cattle figures come from feeding advice for managed cattle, not a yak comparison. The review gives no matching temperature for yaks. The line on yak feed intake and winter weight loss comes from an FAO chapter on yak nutrition.

The genes behind it

Underneath all of this are genes, and researchers have a list of suspects. The leading candidate is EPAS1, a gene that helps the body respond to low oxygen and is believed to influence the hormone that tells it to make more red blood cells. One yak study linked a version of it to higher hemoglobin. Another gene, VEGF-A, is tied to the size of blood vessels.

A 2012 yak genome study, as described in the review, found signs of natural selection in five genes involved in nutrition and metabolism, including one tied to acid secretion in the rumen and one that may help the animal use nitrogen. Four others have been put forward as part of the response to low oxygen.

Comparing the two animals, the review reports that two oxygen-sensing genes, HIF-1α and HIF-2α, were more active in yak tissues than in cattle, and that among the organs studied, the heart differed most between the species.
