Why Yaks Thrive in Thin Air While Cows Collapse

They share a recent ancestor and nearly every protein. But carry a cow up a mountain and its heart may fail, while the yak grazes on as if nothing has changed. Somewhere in that last 1% is the answer.

A swollen brisket

On a high Colorado ranch, a calf stands with its head low and its chest swollen with fluid. The swelling runs from the jaw down through the brisket, the chest beneath the neck. It is the visible sign of a heart giving out. Higher up the same mountains, a yak is grazing.

The two animals are close kin. Yaks and cattle split from a common ancestor only about 4.9 million years ago, and their proteins are more than 99% similar. So why does one collapse where the other thrives?

Sources: Stearns et al., Animals, 2024; Qiu et al., Nature Genetics, 2012

It’s the move, not the cow

Cattle are not doomed at altitude. Among cattle native to high country, 0.5 to 5% get the disease. The problem is the trip. Move cattle above about 1,500 m and the figure jumps to 10 to 40%.

On one Colorado ranch above about 2,400 m, half of the calves that died between branding and weaning died of high-altitude disease. The trouble starts not in the heart but in the lungs.

Sources: Shirley & Garrick, BIF 2007; Merck Veterinary Manual (Holt, CSU); Neary, cited in Chapman et al., Utah Beef Improvement Association report

The pressure gauge

To see it, vets thread a catheter into a cow’s jugular vein and read the pressure in the lung’s main artery. For cattle tested at about 1,700 to 2,100 m, 39 mmHg or lower suggests low risk. A reading of 50 or more means high risk. These cutoffs were set for cattle, not yaks.

A high reading means the heart is pushing blood through arteries that have clamped down.

Source: Colorado State University, via Canadian Cattlemen

The squeeze

Low oxygen makes the small lung arteries of cattle constrict, which raises pressure. Over time the vessel walls thicken, and the heart works harder against that pressure until the right side fails.

A yak’s arteries barely react.

Try it: drag the elevation handle and watch the cattle artery wall thicken while the yak’s stays thin.

Source: Patent background on cattle pulmonary hypertension (US 10138522)

Built differently

Yaks have relatively larger lungs and hearts, and pulmonary arteries with little smooth muscle, the tissue that does the squeezing. In a lab test, low oxygen made cattle artery cells multiply. Yak cells didn’t react significantly.

But was that built by a life at altitude, or handed down in the genes?

Sources: Ayalew et al., Animals, 2021 (review); Zhang et al., Animals, 2024 (PDK1 study)

The herder’s experiment

Yaks and cattle can interbreed, which lets researchers test that question. A cow crossed with a yak gives a dzo, whose lung pressure is yak-like.

Now cross that dzo with a cattle bull. The calves, called stols, split: about half have yak-like lung pressure and half have cow-like. An even split like that points to a single dominant, heritable trait, not something each animal picks up from its surroundings.

The response to thin air is inherited. That leaves the question of where in the DNA it lives.

Source: Anand et al., Thorax, 1986

Where the 1% lives

Comparing the two genomes, researchers found yak gene families expanded for sensing the environment and for energy metabolism, plus selection on genes tied to low oxygen and nutrition, including three hypoxia-response genes selected in yaks but not in cattle.

One gene stands out: EPAS1, also tied to altitude adaptation in Tibetan people. In cattle, 15 of 20 with pulmonary hypertension carried one variant of it. Only 4 of 21 healthy cattle did. That is an association, not proof of cause, but it points to the same switchboard.

The difference between a cow and a yak is not a new organ or a new kind of lung. It is a small set of changes in how a body answers a single alarm. In the cow, thin air sounds it and the arteries clamp down until the heart gives out. In the yak, the alarm rings and the arteries barely stir.

Back on the mountain, the calf and the yak breathe the same air. One of them is simply built to ignore it.

Sources: Qiu et al., Nature Genetics, 2012; Newman et al., Nature Communications, 2015
