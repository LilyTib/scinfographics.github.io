99% the same, yet only one thrives in thin air

Yaks and cattle are about 99% alike genetically, a gap as small as the one between humans and chimpanzees. Yet take a cow high into the mountains and its heart can fail, while a yak just keeps grazing. The reasons run from lungs and arteries all the way down to genes.

A swollen brisket

Some cattle moved to high pastures develop fluid swelling from the head down through the brisket. It is the visible sign of heart failure. Yaks live higher and don’t get it. What’s different?

Source: Stearns et al., Animals, 2024

It’s rare at home, common when moved

Among cattle native to altitude, 0.5 to 5% get the disease. Up to 5% of cattle at or above about 2,000 m die this way in a year.

Sources: Shirley & Garrick, BIF 2007; Merck Veterinary Manual via CSU

Move them up, and it gets worse

For cattle moved above about 1,500 m, the figure is 10 to 40%. On one Colorado ranch above about 2,400 m, half of the calves that died between branding and weaning died of high-altitude disease.

Sources: Shirley & Garrick, BIF 2007; Neary, cited in Utah Beef Improvement Association report

How vets measure a struggling lung

Ranchers thread a catheter into the jugular vein to read the pressure in the lung’s main artery.

Source: Colorado State University, via Canadian Cattlemen

Reading the number (cattle)

For cattle tested at about 1,700 to 2,100 m, 39 mmHg or lower suggests low risk. 50 or more means high risk. These cutoffs were set for cattle, not yaks.

Source: Colorado State University, via Canadian Cattlemen

The squeeze

Low oxygen makes the small lung arteries constrict, which raises pressure. Over time the vessel walls thicken. The heart works harder against that pressure until the right side fails.

Try it: drag the elevation handle and watch the cattle artery wall thicken while the yak’s stays thin.

Source: Patent background on cattle pulmonary hypertension (US 10138522)

Built differently

Yaks have relatively larger lungs and hearts and pulmonary arteries with little smooth muscle. In a lab test, low oxygen made cattle artery cells multiply. Yak cells didn’t react significantly.

Sources: Animals, 2021 review; Animals, 2024 (PDK1 study)

A gene clue

Of 20 cattle with pulmonary hypertension, 15 carried one variant of EPAS1, a gene also tied to Tibetan human altitude adaptation. Only 4 of 21 healthy cattle had it. Yaks carry their own hypoxia-related changes, including selection on three genes that cattle lack.

Sources: US patent 10138522; Qiu et al., Nature Genetics, 2012

The herder’s trick

Cross a cow with a yak and the offspring, called a dzo, has yak-like lung pressure. Cross that dzo with a cattle bull and the calves, called stols, split: about half have yak-like lung pressure and half have cow-like lung pressure. That even split suggests the trait is passed on as a simple dominant.

Source: Anand et al., Thorax, 1986
