Same family, different lungs

Yaks and cattle share almost every gene. At 3,000 metres, one keeps grazing and the other can die of heart failure. Scroll to find out why.

A swollen brisket

Some cattle moved to high pastures develop fluid swelling from the head down through the brisket. It is the visible sign of heart failure. Yaks live higher and don’t get it. What’s different?

Source: Stearns et al., Animals, 2024

It’s rare at home, common when moved

Among cattle native to altitude, 0.5 to 5% get the disease. Up to 5% of cattle at or above 6,500 ft die this way in a year.

Sources: Shirley & Garrick, BIF 2007; Merck Veterinary Manual via CSU

Move them up, and it gets worse

For cattle moved above 5,000 ft, the figure is 10 to 40%. On one Colorado ranch above 8,000 ft, half of the calves that died between branding and weaning died of high-altitude disease.

Sources: Shirley & Garrick, BIF 2007; Neary, cited in Utah Beef Improvement Association report

How vets measure a struggling lung

Ranchers thread a catheter into the jugular vein to read the pressure in the lung’s main artery. At 5,500 to 7,000 ft, 39 mmHg or lower suggests low risk. 50 or more means high risk.

Source: Colorado State University, via Canadian Cattlemen

The squeeze

Low oxygen makes the small lung arteries constrict, which raises pressure. Over time the vessel walls thicken. The heart works harder against that pressure until the right side fails.

Source: Patent background on cattle pulmonary hypertension (US 10138522)

Cattle climb. Yaks stay flat.

In one classic dataset, young cattle averaged about 28 mmHg at sea level and about 35 at 3,048 m. In Ladakh, yaks at 4,500 m had lung pressure no different from yaks bred at low altitude.

Sources: Exp Biol Med (cattle table); Anand et al., Thorax, 1986. Yak values still needed.

Built differently

Yaks have relatively larger lungs and hearts and pulmonary arteries with little smooth muscle. In a lab test, low oxygen made cattle artery cells multiply. Yak cells didn’t react significantly.

Sources: Animals, 2021 review; Animals, 2024 (PDK1 study)

A gene clue

Of 20 cattle with pulmonary hypertension, 15 carried one variant of EPAS1, a gene also tied to Tibetan human altitude adaptation. Only 4 of 21 healthy cattle had it. Yaks carry their own hypoxia-related changes, including selection on three genes that cattle lack.

Sources: US patent 10138522; Qiu et al., Nature Genetics, 2012

The herder’s trick

Cross a cow with a yak and the offspring, called a dzo, has yak-like lung pressure. Cross that dzo with a bull and about half the calves look like yaks and half like cows. That pattern suggests a simple dominant trait.

Source: Anand et al., Thorax, 1986

Selecting for survival

Ranchers now test and cull for low pressure. There is a catch: sires whose offspring resisted the disease had lower weights and gains. And cases now show up in feedlots at elevations down to 3,000 ft.

Sources: J Anim Sci 2008; Utah Beef Improvement Association report
