A tiny fly is making your beef bill even worse

Your beef was already getting pricier. Then a flesh-eating fly closed the border. Here’s how one insect reaches your grocery receipt, link by link.

    So how does a fly end up on your receipt?

    Follow the chain ↓

    Wait — wasn’t beef getting expensive before the fly?

    Yes. The fly landed on a cattle herd that was already running short.

    Prices were climbing for years

    From 2019 to 2024, beef went from $5.70 to $8.40 a pound. No fly in sight yet.

    Because the herd was shrinking

    Southwest droughts in 2020–23 forced ranchers to sell cattle they couldn’t feed. By January 2026 the US herd was down to 86.2 million head, the lowest in 75 years. The 2025 calf crop, 32.9 million, was the smallest since 1941.

    Then the fly reached Mexico

    In November 2024, a screwworm case turned up in southern Mexico. Prices kept rising, to $9.64 by 2026.

    +$2.70 before +$1.24

    About two-thirds of the rise came before the fly reached Mexico. The fly didn’t start the fire. It poured fuel on it.

    What’s so bad about one fly?

    It eats cattle alive, and it was heading straight for the US.

    And it was moving north, fast

    Schematic, not to scale

    2023 · Panama

    The barrier breaks

    For about two decades, a sterile-fly barrier in Panama kept screwworm out of North and Central America. In 2023 it failed: Panama went from about 25 cases a year to more than 6,500.

    2023–24 · Central America

    Country after country

    It spread through Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Honduras, El Salvador, Guatemala and Belize.

    Nov 2024 · Southern Mexico

    It reaches Mexico

    The first Mexican case is detected. This is the moment that matters for your beef bill. Keep reading.

    May 2025 · Oaxaca and Veracruz

    700 miles from the border

    Cases turn up on farms about 700 miles from the US border.

    June 3, 2026 · Texas

    It crosses

    A calf in Zavala County, Texas, is confirmed infested: the first Texas case since 1966.

    Nov 12, 2026 · Texas, New Mexico, Arizona

    128 cases and counting

    USDA has confirmed 128 infested animals across three states, including cattle, horses, dogs and a wild elk.

    So how does a fly in Mexico reach my grocery store?

    It closed the border to about a million cattle a year.

    Mexican cattle crossing into the US

    Before the fly

    A steady stream north

    Every year, Mexico sent about 1.2 million young cattle across the border. They were raised on Mexican ranches and fattened in US feedlots, mostly in the Southern Plains.

    Nov 22, 2024

    One case, gate shut

    Days after the first Mexican case, the US suspended live cattle imports. It reopened briefly in early 2025 under strict inspections.

    May 11, 2025

    Closed again, for over a year

    As the fly kept moving north, the US shut the border again. Imports from Mexico fell to 229,000 head in 2025, down 82%.

    Aug 2026 – now

    A trickle, not a stream

    A phased reopening began in Arizona, then New Mexico, with every animal inspected. By Nov 12, about 83,000 head had crossed.

    The evidence so far

    1. The fly spread into Mexico
    2. The US closed the border to Mexican cattle
    3. About a million cattle a year stopped coming
    4. Fewer cattle to fatten and slaughter
    5. Less beef in the store

    Mexican cattle were only about 3% of US cattle supply, roughly 5% of the cattle entering feedlots. A small slice, but it vanished from a herd already at a 75-year low, with no spare cattle to take its place.

    Can’t ranchers just raise more cattle?

    Not quickly. Cattle aren’t chickens.

    A rancher who sold her breeding cows during the drought can’t restock the market overnight. Every month the border stays shut, that gap gets pushed further into the future.

    Doesn’t a high price make people buy less?

    Barely. Americans keep buying beef, and so does the rest of the world.

    Shoppers trade down, not out

    Even at $9.64 a pound, demand hasn’t collapsed. People switched to cheaper cuts, but the total amount of beef bought held steady.

    Exports compete for the same beef

    Japan, South Korea and China together buy more than 60% of US beef exports, so billions of pounds leave the US supply every year.

    What’s next

    When will it get better?

    Not soon. Here’s what’s being done, and how long it takes.

    1. Now

      Fighting fly with fly

      A Panama facility ships 100 million sterile male flies to Texas each week. Females mate only once, and with a sterile male there are no offspring. The same method wiped out screwworm in the US in the 1960s.

    2. Since Aug 2026

      The border reopens, slowly

      Crossings are reopening one at a time, with Nogales, Arizona, set for Nov 30. Economists say it will take months for imports to return to normal, and they don’t expect a quick drop in prices.

    3. Pending

      NovoFly

      A genetically modified fly that produces only males, which would double sterile-fly output. The EPA is still reviewing it.

    4. Nov 2027

      A US fly factory

      A $750 million plant at Moore Air Base in Edinburg, Texas, the first in the US, starts at 100 million flies a week and scales to 300 million.

    5. Years

      Rebuilding the herd

      Even with the fly under control, it takes years to raise enough cattle. A University of Illinois model finds that if imports stay at today’s pace, prices for fattened cattle in 2029 would be about 4% higher than if trade fully recovers.
